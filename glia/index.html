Glia is a place to keep the texture of a life: stories, keepsakes, and the people they belong to. You compose digital shoeboxes of moments and gift them. Underneath is a privacy-first memory system that pays attention over time, so conversations and stray thoughts can become something you can hold, return to, and share.

I founded the company in 2025 and work the full cycle of design, research, engineering, and marketing to bring a futuristic yet humanistic concept to life. Ambient AI design is a big experiment Glia pioneered: the interface language, the memory model, and the experience of composing a shoebox with someone else. The product is live as an iOS app and on the web.

The belief

Memory has always been about stories and keepsakes: dried flowers, a boarding pass, letters and postcards, photos of toothy smiles, a grandmother’s recipe written down by hand. Most software treats memory as content to scroll past.

Every culture has built practices for preserving what matters: oral traditions, rites of passage, the careful curation of objects and photographs and shared meals. Those were technologies of meaning, ways of saying this moment counted, this person mattered, this is the shape of my life. Glia is built in that tradition: composing attention into a record of a relationship, rather than a feed.

How do you design technology that helps people remember, relive, and reflect with the people they care about, without turning intimacy into a feed?

Where it came from

The idea surfaced in Shanghai in summer 2024, during a visit to my aunt’s: a city so relentlessly alive it makes you feel like you’re always on the edge of missing something. The name comes from glial cells, the connective tissue of the brain that holds everything together without taking the credit.

An earlier product exploration, Buttercup, was framed around lifelong learning. In research, something else kept surfacing: people recording conversations with family not to study anything, but so they could understand each other better through what they remembered together. That ethnographic thread (memory as relationship, not curriculum) is what Glia grew from.

The question I keep returning to is how to engineer and design empathy inside a piece of technology.

What I designed and built

The product is organized around people, place, and stories: the grammar of a life. You curate shoeboxes of keepsakes, small on purpose (playing favorites is the point), and give them to someone. Conversations and stray thoughts become artifacts: a dinner that tasted like home, a friend’s news, a walk after rain.

I designed the interface to behave more like a good friend than a feed. It holds space, listens to what is said and unsaid, and does not demand attention several times a day. When you spiral, it can gently challenge. The rest of the time, it pays attention. That is the ambient AI experiment: intelligence that stays with a life without asking to be the center of the day. The commitments underneath are poetic computing, an organic interface that grows with you, and the belief that attention is all we have. Spend it on what matters.

Memory, AI, and human-centric design

Memory architecture

Most products remember a chat log. Glia is designed to remember a day in a life. A lot of the research and engineering is long-term memory: how an AI can actually know who you are, not just what you typed in the last session. Conversations, notes, photos, and the rest are treated as ears, not as separate products. They land in one ledger: episodes with people, place, and what actually happened. A retrieval index sits on top of that (semantic search, keywords, and recency together) so a scene can be found later without turning the whole life into a feed.

The composing model is the ethos made structural: dot to line to story. Evidence first, then structure, then narrative, never the other way around. Moments freeze once they are composed. A keepsake is one bounded scene, held as the person’s own words plus a witness read of the context, not a pile of quotes ripped out of time.

When something is handed back, it should be a postcard, not a newspaper.

AI architecture

Ambient AI design is that experiment made into a system: not a chat box waiting to be prompted, but something that can live in the background of a day and speak only when it has something worth handing back. Each chat turn is read once for subtext, stance, and how much memory to bring in. Retrieval is allowed to be wide; what reaches the reply is slim: a few atomic facts for this beat, not a dossier. The model is a friend with long-term memory, not a coach: it only knows you if that memory work underneath can hold who you are over time. Replies stay short on purpose. If someone already named their next move, the system stops. If they are hurting, it receives first and does not assign homework.

Language models polish summaries and compose stories, but they do not get to decide whether a memory exists. Existence is evidence. Quality gates sit after compose. The system is also designed to stay silent: handing the wrong scene back is worse than handing nothing.

Human-centric design

The hardest design work is epistemic. Fragments of a life are not a proof. Two days may rhyme; they are not a syllogism. The architecture is not allowed to invent cause. It can place two evidenced scenes next to each other and leave the because to the person.

A memory is only gifted back from someone’s own voice: a caption, a journal line, something they said, not from a stats import. Ambient photo listening and contacts are opt-in, and contacts never mean the full address book. People pages are built to answer why this person matters: what they say, what you did together, not an inventory of story cards.

Attention is the design constraint. Spend it on what matters, including the choice not to speak.

In public

Glia has been featured in Dazed, and connected to public showcases including the Southbank Centre’s Creative Intelligence Festival and Tate Modern’s Evolving Studio programme. In London I also run The Wonder Lab, Glia’s offline community for creative work.
