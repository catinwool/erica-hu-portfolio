Glia: ambient AI for memory and friendship

Founder

Venture-backed startup pioneering a privacy-first memory composer for stories, keepsakes, and the people they belong to.

I founded the company in 2025. The product is live as an iOS app and on the web.

Filmed, produced, written by Erica Hu

The belief

Memory has always been about stories and keepsakes: dried flowers, a boarding pass, a grandmother’s recipe written down by hand. We have always known how to remember a life as a shoebox. Most software turns that into an inbox: life scattered across platforms, saved without end, and shown to people far outside the ones you actually know.

How do you design technology that helps people remember, relive, and reflect with the people they care about, without turning intimacy into a feed?

The idea surfaced in Shanghai in summer 2024, during a visit to my aunt’s. The name comes from glial cells, the connective tissue of the brain that holds everything together without taking the credit.

An earlier exploration, Buttercup, was framed around learning. In research, people kept recording conversations with family so they could understand each other better through what they remembered together. That thread is what Glia grew from.

Remember the bright notes of daisies saturated by the rain? @gliahq

Ambient collector

Glia sits in the corners of the internet you already use. Films, quotes, music, runs, and books come in as keepsakes.

Personal curator

It learns what carries sentimental value and keeps only what matters. The box stays small on purpose.

Remember together

A box is something you give. Memory is for the people it belongs to.

The interface is meant to behave more like a good friend than a feed. It holds space, listens to what is said and unsaid, and does not demand attention several times a day. That is the ambient AI experiment: intelligence that stays with a life without asking to be the center of the day.

Most products remember a chat log. Glia is designed to remember a day in a life. Language models polish summaries, but they do not get to decide whether a memory exists. When something is handed back, it should be a postcard, not a newspaper.
